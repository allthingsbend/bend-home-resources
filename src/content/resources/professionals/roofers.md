## Repair, replace or monitor?

Start with the problem, not the product a salesperson wants to sell.

<div class="agent-guide-grid">
  <div class="agent-guide-card"><strong>Repair may fit</strong><span>The defect is isolated, the surrounding roof is serviceable, compatible materials are available and the repair can be warranted.</span></div>
  <div class="agent-guide-card"><strong>Replacement may fit</strong><span>Deterioration is widespread, failures recur, the system is brittle or patched extensively, or the remaining life no longer justifies repeated work.</span></div>
  <div class="agent-guide-card"><strong>Monitor may fit</strong><span>No active failure is found, maintenance is manageable and documented inspections can track a known but non-urgent condition.</span></div>
</div>

### Evidence that matters

Ask the roofer to photograph and label the findings. Useful evidence can include:

- Missing, lifted, cracked or mechanically damaged materials.
- Granule loss or surface wear unusual for the roof's age and exposure.
- Failed pipe boots, skylight curbs, chimney flashing, sidewall flashing or roof-to-wall transitions.
- Soft, stained or delaminated sheathing visible from the attic or roof edge.
- Recurring leakage in more than one area.
- Multiple roofing layers or repairs that limit another durable repair.
- Fastener, seam or coating problems on metal or low-slope systems.
- Inadequate drainage, ponding or edge details on a low-slope roof.
- Ventilation or condensation evidence that can imitate a roof leak.

An estimated age alone does not prove failure, and a leak does not automatically require a full replacement. Ask: **Where is water entering? What failed? What portion remains serviceable? What would the proposed repair accomplish, and what would it not fix?**

If two roofers give opposite recommendations, pay for an independent inspection by a qualified professional who is not bidding the replacement, or ask both firms to address the other explanation in writing.

### Active leak: what to do first

Protect people and the interior. Move belongings if safe, contain water, photograph damage and contact the insurer when a claim may apply. Do not climb onto a wet, icy, steep or damaged roof. An emergency tarp or temporary seal is damage control, not necessarily the permanent repair.

Document the temporary work, who performed it and what permanent work remains. Water can travel along decking, framing or underlayment before appearing indoors, so the ceiling stain may not be directly below the exterior defect. See the [water-damage repair guide](/costs/water-damage-repair) when insulation, drywall, flooring or framing is wet.

## What a complete roof replacement bid should include

A one-page total labeled “new roof” is not enough. Give every bidder the same requested scope and require substitutions or exclusions to be obvious.

| Bid section | What should be stated |
| --- | --- |
| **Existing conditions** | Roof dimensions or squares, pitch, number of layers, material, access constraints and known damage. |
| **Tear-off** | Layers included, disposal, landscaping protection and how the structure will be dried in if weather changes. |
| **Decking** | Inspection method, included allowance, unit price per sheet or square foot, approval process and photo documentation. |
| **Roof system** | Manufacturer, product line, color, underlayment, starter, field material, ridge or hip cap and accessories. |
| **Water management** | Eaves, valleys, ice barrier, drip edge, wall and chimney flashing, kick-out flashing and low-slope transitions. |
| **Penetrations** | Plumbing and exhaust vents, skylights, chimneys, antennas, satellite equipment and solar coordination. |
| **Ventilation** | Existing intake and exhaust, proposed changes, rationale and attic work excluded from the roof contract. |
| **Gutters and fascia** | Removal, reset or replacement; responsibility for fascia, apron flashing, downspouts and cleanup. |
| **Permits** | Who applies and pays, required inspections and who provides the closed permit record. |
| **Site and cleanup** | Driveway access, debris containment, daily cleanup, nail sweep and protection for the property. |
| **Schedule and payment** | Estimated start, weather handling, duration, deposit, progress payments, final payment and lien documents. |
| **Warranties** | Contractor workmanship terms, manufacturer coverage, registration, transfer rules and exclusions. |

### The concealed-damage clause

Decking and framing damage may not be visible until the old roof is removed. A fair contract explains the process before that happens:

1. The crew exposes a condition outside the base scope.
2. Work pauses in that area if it can safely pause.
3. The contractor supplies photos, quantity and the agreed unit price.
4. The homeowner authorizes the change in writing.
5. The final invoice identifies the actual added quantity.

“Rot extra” with no unit price or authorization process leaves too much uncertainty. Also ask what happens if damage is structural and requires a carpenter, engineer or additional permit review.

One proposal may include permits, upgraded underlayment, all new flashing and a decking allowance while another excludes them. Add known exclusions and reasonable allowances before comparing totals. The [contractor bid comparison tool](/tools/contractor-bid-comparison) can help.

## Roofing materials for Central Oregon homes

Material selection is a system decision. Roof shape, slope, structure, wildfire requirements, snow shedding, trees, solar plans, budget, appearance and installer quality all matter.

### Architectural asphalt shingles

Architectural composition shingles are common, familiar to many installers and usually less expensive initially than premium metal or composite systems. Compare:

- Product line and published wind, fire and impact classifications.
- Required starter, cap and underlayment components.
- Nail pattern and whether high-wind installation requirements apply.
- Manufacturer credential requirements for the offered warranty tier.
- Color, heat exposure and HOA restrictions.

Do not compare “30-year” and “50-year” labels as if they predict an exact service life. Read what the warranty covers, how coverage changes over time and whether labor, tear-off and disposal are included.

### Standing-seam and exposed-fastener metal

Metal is not one product. Standing-seam panels usually conceal fasteners in the field; exposed-fastener panels rely on fasteners and washers that remain part of the weather surface. Panel thickness, metal type, coating, seam profile, clip design, substrate and details all affect performance.

Ask about the panel manufacturer and coating; deck or batten assembly; expansion and contraction; valleys, eaves, walls and penetrations; snow retention above doors and walks; future solar access; and whether the crew fabricates trim.

Metal can shed snow suddenly. The right snow-retention plan is site-specific; adding devices later can be harder and may affect the roof warranty.

### Composite, low-slope and unusual systems

Composite products can imitate slate or shake without matching their weight or maintenance profile. Confirm the exact product's approved slope, fire classification, fastening, underlayment and local availability.

TPO, PVC, EPDM and modified-bitumen low-slope systems have different seam, flashing and repair methods. Drainage, substrate, insulation, attachment, perimeter securement and penetrations are critical. Use a contractor with documented experience in the exact system.

Tile is heavy and needs compatible structure and flashing expertise. Wood shakes or shingles raise maintenance, insurance, wildfire and local-code questions. Before changing material type, confirm structural capacity, jurisdiction requirements, HOA rules and insurance acceptability.

## The details that prevent many leaks

The visible roof covering gets the attention. Transitions usually deserve more questions.

### Flashing

Ask whether each area will be replaced, reused or repaired:

- Step and counterflashing at walls and chimneys.
- Headwall, apron and kick-out flashing.
- Valley metal or the manufacturer's approved valley assembly.
- Skylight kits, curbs, plumbing boots and mechanical penetrations.
- Roof-to-siding transitions and siding removal needed for correct integration.

“Reuse existing flashing as needed” is not a complete plan. If flashing disappears behind siding or masonry, establish who opens and restores that assembly.

### Decking, underlayment and ventilation

The deck must provide a sound fastening surface. Underlayment is a secondary water-shedding layer, not permission to ignore surface details. If sheathing is exposed during a Bend reroof, the City's current guide calls for ice barrier and flashing. Require the contractor to state where each underlayment type goes and how the roof will be protected overnight.

A roofer may evaluate intake and exhaust vents, but attic moisture and ice problems can involve air leakage, insulation, bath-fan discharge and indoor humidity. More roof vents do not automatically solve an unbalanced system. If condensation or ice dams recur, consider an attic or building-performance specialist too.

## Bend conditions to design around

Bend's high-desert setting exposes roofs to strong sun, temperature swings, wind, snow, ice, airborne debris and occasional concentrated moisture. Site conditions vary dramatically across town.

- **Sun and heat:** South- and west-facing planes may age differently than shaded slopes. Product choice and attic conditions both matter.
- **Snow and ice:** Valleys, north-facing areas, low-slope transitions and heat-loss patterns can concentrate risk. Keep snow-shedding paths away from entries and equipment.
- **Wind:** Edge securement, starter, fastener placement, panel design and exposed locations deserve explicit discussion.
- **Trees and debris:** Needles and branches can retain moisture, clog valleys and gutters, abrade surfaces or create impact risk.
- **Wildfire:** Roof covering, edges, valleys, vents and accumulated debris are part of home hardening. No roof makes a home fireproof.
- **Moss and growth:** Shade and debris can create localized conditions even in a dry climate. Avoid aggressive cleaning that damages the roofing surface.

Ask each roofer to walk the property and explain which exposures changed the proposed system. A generic product pitch that would be identical in any climate is less useful than a site-specific plan.

## Coordinate roof, siding, gutters, solar and chimneys

Exterior projects overlap. Decide ownership at every handoff before contracts are signed.

### Siding and roof-to-wall flashing

When water is getting behind siding near a roof intersection, replacing shingles alone may preserve the defect. A correct repair may require temporarily removing siding, installing step or headwall flashing and a kick-out, repairing sheathing, then restoring the wall's weather-resistive barrier and siding.

Name the contractor responsible for each layer. If two companies are involved, require them to agree on sequence and warranty boundaries in writing.

### Gutters, fascia and drainage

New roof edge metal must work with the gutter. Decide whether gutters stay, are removed and reset, or are replaced after roofing. Inspect fascia while it is accessible and route downspouts to a safe discharge location. The [Bend gutter company guide](/professionals/gutter-companies) covers sizing, drainage and contractor comparison.

### Solar panels

Identify who removes, stores and reinstalls panels, who handles electrical and structural permits, and whether the original solar installer must participate to preserve coverage. Coordinate mounts and flashing with the roofing manufacturer and installer. The City of Bend notes that solar-panel work associated with reroofing may require electrical and, depending on the installation, structural permitting.

### Chimneys, skylights and rooftop equipment

A roofer can flash these elements but may not repair the underlying chimney, skylight assembly or mechanical equipment. Have deteriorated masonry, failed skylights and obsolete vents evaluated early. Replacing them during reroofing can avoid disturbing a new system later.

## Oregon licensing, contracts and Bend permits

Oregon requires contractors performing roofing and most paid construction work to hold the appropriate CCB license. Verify it yourself even when the company came from a trusted referral.

### License check

Before signing or paying:

1. Search the exact business in the **[Oregon CCB database](https://search.ccb.state.or.us/search/)**.
2. Confirm active status and that the name on the bid matches the license.
3. Review endorsement, bond, insurance and history.
4. Ask who the employees and subcontractors on site will work for.
5. Recheck shortly before work begins if the project was scheduled far ahead.

A valid license is a minimum requirement, not a quality award. References, relevant system experience, crew supervision and contract quality still matter.

### Written contract

Oregon CCB consumer guidance calls for detailed written contracts and notes that contracts are required for construction work over the state's threshold. A roofing contract should identify the parties and jobsite, CCB number, full scope and products, price and payment schedule, start and completion expectations, permit responsibility, change-order process, warranties and signatures.

Do not pay the entire project in advance or use cash. Tie payments to clear milestones and retain documentation. Ask about conditional or final lien releases when subcontractors or suppliers are involved.

### Permits and jurisdiction

Do not assume “Bend address” means City of Bend jurisdiction; some properties are in unincorporated Deschutes County. Confirm the authority for the exact parcel.

The **[City of Bend reroofing guide](https://bendoregon.gov/services/permits-licenses/property-owners/reroofing/)** identifies common permit triggers, including changing roofing material, structural changes or new sheathing, and Bend's wildfire-hazard requirements. It also explains plan and inspection steps. Fees, processing and forms can change, so use the live guide rather than an old quote.

State wildfire provisions and local adoption are nuanced. Deschutes County and the City of Sisters currently describe R327 requirements for specified new dwellings and accessory structures, while partial repairs and remodels are treated differently. Do not let a contractor apply one jurisdiction's summary to every existing Bend reroof; ask the building department what governs your address and scope.

Put in the contract who obtains the permit, schedules inspections, corrects deficiencies and supplies final approval. A permit is not a substitute for your own contract or quality control.

## Insurance claims and roof records

Coverage depends on your policy, endorsements, deductible, cause of loss and roof settlement terms. Age or wear alone does not create a covered claim.

If storm or impact damage may be covered:

- Photograph conditions before temporary work when it is safe.
- Prevent additional damage without making unsafe repairs.
- Notify the insurer promptly and follow its documentation instructions.
- Keep emergency invoices, inspection photos and correspondence.
- Ask the contractor to describe physical damage without promising what insurance will pay.
- Compare the insurer's scope and contractor's scope line by line.
- Require written authorization for supplements and upgrades.
- Be cautious about assigning broad claim rights without understanding the document.

After any repair or replacement, keep the signed contract, change orders, invoices, proof of payment, permit and final inspection, product names and colors, warranty registration, photographs and contractor contact information. These records can help with future repairs, warranty claims, underwriting and resale.

## Understand the warranties before choosing a bid

“Lifetime roof warranty” is incomplete without the actual documents.

### Manufacturer coverage

Manufacturer warranties generally address qualifying product defects under stated conditions. Enhanced system coverage may require approved accessories, installation methods, credentialed contractors and timely registration. Ask whether labor, tear-off, disposal and replacement accessories are covered, and how coverage changes over time.

### Workmanship coverage

The contractor's warranty addresses installation work and is only as useful as its written terms and the company's ability to respond. Ask:

- Exact duration and covered defects.
- Response process and expected timing.
- Whether repairs by another contractor void coverage.
- Exclusions for storms, movement, condensation, other trades and owner maintenance.
- Transfer rules after a sale.
- Responsibility for interior damage caused by a covered installation defect.

Solar installers, satellite technicians, chimney contractors and pressure washers can affect a roof. Ask how future penetrations must be handled and document any later work. Keep valleys and drainage paths clear using methods approved for the material.

## A 100-point roofer scorecard

Score every serious candidate from the written proposal and interview—not from memory.

| Category | Points | What earns a strong score |
| --- | ---: | --- |
| **License and insurance verification** | 15 | Active matching CCB record; current coverage; clear employee/subcontractor structure. |
| **Relevant roof-system experience** | 15 | Recent comparable projects, knowledgeable estimator and trained installation crew. |
| **Diagnosis and site analysis** | 15 | Photos, clear explanation, repair alternatives where viable and Bend-specific detailing. |
| **Scope completeness** | 20 | Exact system, tear-off, decking, flashing, ventilation, penetrations, permits and cleanup. |
| **Price and change-order clarity** | 10 | Transparent allowances, unit prices, milestones, exclusions and written approvals. |
| **Warranty quality** | 10 | Actual documents, realistic coverage, registration and a clear service process. |
| **Communication and supervision** | 10 | Named project contact, crew oversight, schedule updates and prompt answers. |
| **References and recordkeeping** | 5 | Comparable references and commitment to deliver closeout documents. |
| **Total** | **100** | Compare fit and risk—not merely the highest arithmetic score. |

Do not award points for a low total until the scope is normalized. A company can earn a strong score and still be the wrong fit if it rarely installs your chosen system or cannot meet a critical schedule.

## Questions to ask every Bend roofer

### Company and crew

1. What is the exact legal business name and Oregon CCB number?
2. Who will estimate, supervise and perform the work?
3. Are installers employees or subcontractors, and who covers them?
4. How many recent projects used this exact roof system?
5. Can I contact two customers with similar homes or details?
6. What work do you exclude or refer to another trade?

### Diagnosis and scope

7. What failed, and what evidence supports repair or replacement?
8. If a repair is viable, what life and limitations should I reasonably expect?
9. How many layers will be removed?
10. How will you inspect and price damaged decking?
11. Which flashing will be replaced, reused or custom fabricated?
12. Where will ice barrier and each underlayment be installed?
13. How will valleys, sidewalls, chimneys, skylights and low-slope transitions be built?
14. What ventilation changes are proposed, and why?
15. How will the roof be dried in if weather interrupts the job?

### Logistics and protection

16. Who obtains and closes the permit?
17. What is the realistic schedule, and what can change it?
18. How will you protect siding, windows, landscaping, decks, HVAC equipment and neighbors?
19. Where will the dumpster, trailer and materials go?
20. How will nails and debris be removed each day and at completion?
21. Who handles gutters, fascia, solar, satellite equipment and other rooftop systems?

### Money, warranties and completion

22. What is excluded from this price?
23. What allowances or unit prices could change the total?
24. What deposit and progress payments are required?
25. How are change orders documented and approved?
26. May I review the manufacturer and workmanship warranties now?
27. Who registers the warranty and provides confirmation?
28. What documents will I receive before final payment?
29. How are punch-list and post-completion leak calls handled?
30. What maintenance is required to keep coverage valid?

## Red flags and high-pressure tactics

Pause when you encounter:

- An Oregon CCB number that is missing, inactive or does not match the contracting business.
- Pressure to sign during the first visit or immediately after a storm.
- A promise that insurance will “definitely pay” before the insurer evaluates coverage.
- A large cash payment or full payment before work begins.
- A bid with no product lines, flashing detail, decking price or exclusions.
- Instructions to obtain an owner permit when the contractor is actually responsible for the work.
- Refusal to supply warranty documents before signing.
- A dramatically low bid explained only as a temporary discount.
- Vague answers about who supervises the crew or performs specialized metal and membrane details.
- A request to sign over insurance proceeds or claim authority you do not understand.
- No plan for protecting the home if the roof is open and weather changes.
- A sales presentation focused on warranty length while avoiding the exclusions.

Oregon CCB guidance recommends checking the license and complaint history, obtaining multiple bids, using a detailed written contract and avoiding full advance payment. A polished salesperson or trusted referral does not replace those checks.

## Project day and final walkthrough

Before work begins, move vehicles, protect fragile items in the attic and on walls, mark irrigation or delicate landscaping, arrange pet and child safety, and confirm power, driveway and bathroom expectations. Roofing is noisy; warn occupants and nearby neighbors.

During the project, keep decisions with the named supervisor. Photograph concealed conditions and approved changes. Do not direct individual crew members to add work outside the contract.

Before final payment:

- Confirm the agreed material, color and visible details.
- Review photos of decking, underlayment and concealed flashing.
- Check gutters, downspouts, siding, vents, skylights and roof-to-wall transitions.
- Confirm debris and fasteners were removed from landscaping, driveway, attic and gutters.
- Test or inspect any equipment disconnected or moved.
- Resolve the written punch list.
- Receive the final invoice, paid receipt, warranties, registration, permit closeout, product information and lien documentation as appropriate.
