## Start here: the 10-minute shortlist

You do not need to identify the single most famous real estate agent in Bend. You need to find two or three qualified candidates and compare them using the same criteria.

<div class="agent-guide-grid">
  <div class="agent-guide-card"><strong>Buying in Bend</strong><span>Prioritize property analysis, offer strategy, inspection coordination, availability and clear explanations of risk.</span></div>
  <div class="agent-guide-card"><strong>Selling in Bend</strong><span>Prioritize pricing evidence, preparation advice, marketing execution, offer comparison and a realistic communication plan.</span></div>
  <div class="agent-guide-card"><strong>Relocating to Bend</strong><span>Prioritize remote-showing systems, objective area research, vendor coordination and experience with your property type.</span></div>
</div>

For each candidate, complete these six checks:

1. **Verify the Oregon license.** Confirm that the person has an active broker or principal broker license and identify the real estate business where the license is registered.
2. **Confirm relevant experience.** Ask about recent work with your price range, property type and side of the transaction—not just total years in real estate.
3. **Interview the person who will do the work.** If you are hiring a team, find out who answers your calls, attends showings, writes offers, reviews documents and handles problems.
4. **Request the agreement before committing.** Read the duration, exclusivity, geographic scope, compensation and termination language.
5. **Compare the actual process.** Ask each candidate to explain how they evaluate a property or prepare a listing, not simply how hard they work.
6. **Check communication fit.** Agree on normal response times, urgent communication, update frequency and backup coverage.

<div class="checklist-box"><strong>A useful rule:</strong> Choose the agent who improves the quality of your decisions. Access to listings, a polished presentation and a friendly personality matter, but they are not substitutes for judgment, organization and candor.</div>

This page is an independent selection guide, not a paid ranking of individual Bend agents. A directory listing, online review or professional membership should be treated as one input—not an automatic endorsement.

## Real estate agent, broker, principal broker and REALTOR®

These terms are often mixed together in everyday conversation, but they do not mean exactly the same thing.

| Term | What it generally means in Oregon | What to verify |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Real estate agent** | A common consumer term for someone licensed to help with real estate transactions. Oregon's license title is generally broker or principal broker. | Active Oregon license, registered business and disciplinary information. |
| **Broker** | An Oregon real estate licensee who conducts professional real estate activity under the supervision and control required by state law. | License status, license number and supervising principal broker. |
| **Principal broker** | A higher Oregon license level that can include supervisory responsibilities. | Whether the person is supervising others, who manages the business and who is responsible for your transaction. |
| **REALTOR®** | A licensed real estate professional who is also a member of the National Association of REALTORS® and subscribes to its Code of Ethics. | State license first; then membership if the designation matters to you. |
| **Real estate team** | A group of licensees working together within the structure allowed by Oregon law. | Team disclosure, individual roles, coverage and which person you are actually hiring. |

The Oregon Real Estate Agency—not a review platform, brokerage website or trade association—controls state licensing. Use the Agency's official **License Lookup** to confirm the person and business before signing an agreement or sending sensitive financial information.

A license confirms legal eligibility to practice. It does not tell you whether the agent is responsive, skilled with older homes, strong at pricing, experienced with HOAs or a good match for your communication style. That requires a separate evaluation.

## What a strong Bend buyer's agent should actually do

A buyer's agent should contribute more than a search portal and access to a lockbox. The value should appear throughout the transaction.

### Before you tour homes

A strong agent should help you:

- Translate a broad wish list into clear priorities and acceptable tradeoffs.
- Coordinate timing with your lender and encourage a current preapproval before competitive shopping.
- Explain how price, interest rate, taxes, insurance, HOA dues and likely maintenance affect the usable budget.
- Separate non-negotiable needs from preferences that can change after seeing the local inventory.
- Set up a search without filtering out reasonable options merely because a listing is poorly written or photographed.
- Explain the buyer representation agreement before asking for a signature.

Start with the [Bend mortgage preapproval guide](/buying/getting-pre-approved) if financing is not yet organized.

### During showings

The agent is not the home inspector, structural engineer, attorney or insurance underwriter. A good agent still helps you notice questions that deserve more research.

That can include:

- Signs that an addition, conversion or major project may need a permit-record check.
- Roof age, drainage patterns, siding exposure and other visible items to investigate.
- Whether an HOA, shared drive, private road, irrigation right, easement or access issue may affect the property.
- Whether the property has municipal services or requires research into a well, septic system or other private infrastructure.
- How the lot, topography, trees, snow storage and sun exposure could affect ownership.
- Whether your intended use appears to require land-use, HOA, insurance or lender confirmation.

The right response is often, “We need to verify that,” followed by a plan and the correct source. Be cautious of an agent who confidently diagnoses a technical issue outside the agent's expertise.

### Before an offer

A strong agent should prepare and explain an evidence-based pricing analysis. That normally includes relevant closed sales, pending competition when knowable, active alternatives, condition differences, location factors and the limits of the data.

The agent should also help you understand:

- Price and escalation strategy.
- Earnest money and key deadlines.
- Financing, appraisal and inspection protections.
- Seller credits, personal property and repair language.
- Possession timing and any seller occupancy after closing.
- The practical risk of changing or waiving a contingency.
- What information is known, unknown or still needs verification.

“This is what wins” is not a complete explanation. You should understand what each term may improve, what protection it reduces and whether the tradeoff fits your risk tolerance. See the full [guide to making an offer in Bend](/buying/making-an-offer).

### During due diligence

Once an offer is accepted, organization matters. A capable agent should maintain a calendar of contractual deadlines, help you arrange the appropriate inspections, obtain documents promptly and coordinate requests for specialist opinions.

That can include:

- General home inspection and any justified specialist follow-up.
- Sewer scope, septic, well, radon, mold, roof, chimney, HVAC or structural evaluation when relevant.
- Seller disclosures and updates.
- Title report, easements and exceptions that need questions.
- HOA documents, budgets, assessments, insurance information, litigation and use restrictions.
- Permit and property-record research.
- Insurance availability and property-specific underwriting questions.
- Repair estimates needed before a response deadline.

The agent should help you manage the process without pretending to replace the qualified professionals. Use the [Bend home inspection guide](/buying/home-inspections) and [inspection checklist](/tools/home-inspection-checklist) to build the right scope.

### From negotiation to closing

After inspections, a strong agent should help distinguish material concerns from minor maintenance, organize evidence, explain negotiation options and write a clear response. Later, the agent should track appraisal, lending, title, escrow, signing, funds, possession and the [final walkthrough](/buying/final-walkthrough).

You should know what remains open, who owns each task and when a decision is required. An agent does not control every participant, but the agent should notice gaps early and keep the transaction moving.

## The Bend knowledge that matters most

“Local expert” should mean more than living in Central Oregon or knowing restaurant recommendations. Ask candidates to show how local experience changes the questions they ask and the professionals they involve.

### Property type and location

Bend includes newer subdivisions, older central neighborhoods, steep west-side lots, planned communities, golf communities, homes near open space, acreage at the edge of town and properties outside city limits that still use a Bend mailing address.

Relevant experience may include:

- Homes inside the City of Bend versus unincorporated Deschutes County.
- Older homes with additions, conversions or incomplete records.
- New construction, builder contracts and warranty procedures.
- Condominiums, townhomes and layered HOA structures.
- Rural or semi-rural properties with wells, septic systems, irrigation or private roads.
- Luxury homes, investment properties or short-term-rental considerations.
- Homes with wildfire exposure or insurance constraints.

An agent does not need to have sold on the exact block. The agent should be able to explain what is different about the property category and how the due-diligence plan changes.

### Central Oregon ownership issues

A thoughtful Bend agent should recognize when to investigate:

- **Wildfire and insurance:** defensible space, vegetation, roof and siding materials, nearby fuels, access and property-specific insurability.
- **Snow, ice and freeze-thaw:** roof drainage, north-facing surfaces, steep drives, snow storage and winter access.
- **Water and drainage:** grading, roof runoff, irrigation, crawlspace moisture and where snowmelt goes.
- **Exterior maintenance:** roofs, siding, flashing, decks, paint and sun exposure.
- **Utilities and services:** sewer, septic, water source, internet availability and service boundaries.
- **HOAs and shared obligations:** assessments, reserves, restrictions, insurance deductibles, common property, private roads and pending disputes.
- **Permits and intended use:** finished spaces, additions, accessory structures, rentals and other uses that require confirmation.

The agent's job is not to guarantee that these items are acceptable. The useful skill is identifying the issue, explaining why it matters, locating the relevant documents and helping you get qualified advice before a deadline expires.

### Neighborhood guidance without steering

Tell the agent what you value in concrete terms: maximum commute, lot size, trail access, noise tolerance, home age, topography, walkability, snow exposure or proximity to a specific place.

An ethical agent should direct you to objective sources for schools, crime, transportation, land use and demographics rather than label communities as “good,” “safe,” “family” or “up-and-coming” based on protected characteristics. This protects fair-housing rights and usually produces a better decision because the criteria come from you.

## How to find credible candidates

Use several sources to build a shortlist. No single source measures the full job.

### Referrals

Ask recent Bend buyers or sellers what the agent did when the transaction became difficult. “We loved our agent” is encouraging, but details are more useful:

- Did the agent identify or organize a hard property question?
- Was the pricing or offer advice supported by evidence?
- Did communication stay consistent after the contract was signed?
- Who actually handled showings and documents?
- How did the agent respond to an inspection, appraisal or title problem?

A referral from another city can still be useful, but interview the Bend agent independently. Referral relationships may include a fee between brokerages, so do not treat the introduction as proof of fit.

### Official license and membership sources

Use the Oregon Real Estate Agency to verify licensing. If a candidate uses the REALTOR® designation, membership resources from the National Association of REALTORS® or the local Cascades East Association of REALTORS® can provide additional context.

Professional designations may signal training in a niche, but the initials should connect to your needs. Ask what the training changed about the person's work.

### Online reviews

Reviews can reveal patterns, especially when comments repeatedly mention communication, problem-solving and transaction type. They also have limits:

- A high star rating can come from a small or selective sample.
- Reviews often describe personality more than technical performance.
- Transaction complexity and client expectations vary.
- Old reviews may describe a different team or market.
- A few negative reviews should be read for the agent's response and the pattern, not just the score.

Use reviews to develop interview questions, not to skip the interview.

### Listings, marketing and public presence

For a listing agent, examine several current and sold listings. Look at photography, descriptions, floor plans, data accuracy and whether the marketing fits the property. For a buyer's agent, public marketing tells you less about the behind-the-scenes work, so ask for an anonymized example of the agent's process or a sample timeline instead.

## Interview two or three agents

Use the same core questions for every candidate. The quality of the explanation matters as much as the answer.

### Experience and fit

1. How many buyers or sellers similar to me have you represented in the last 12 months?
2. What property types and areas make up most of your recent Bend work?
3. What kinds of transactions are not a strong fit for you?
4. Are you a solo agent or part of a team, and who would handle each part of my transaction?
5. How many active clients are you personally managing right now?
6. Who covers if you are unavailable?

### Process and property analysis

7. What do you do before recommending an offer price or listing price?
8. Walk me through how you evaluate a house during a showing.
9. How do you help clients research permits, HOAs, title questions or property-specific risks?
10. What is your process when an inspection reveals a major issue?
11. How do you coordinate specialist opinions and repair estimates within a short deadline?
12. Give me an anonymized example of a transaction where your first plan had to change.

### Offers, pricing and negotiation

13. How do you distinguish a comparable sale from a misleading comp?
14. How do you explain the tradeoffs among price, credits, contingencies, appraisal protection and timing?
15. When would you advise a buyer not to waive a protection?
16. How do you present multiple offers to a seller so the seller can compare net value and risk?
17. How do you handle a low appraisal or a repair negotiation?

### Communication and expectations

18. What is your normal response time, and what counts as urgent?
19. Will I communicate primarily by text, phone, email or a shared system?
20. How often will you update me when there is no major news?
21. Who sends deadline reminders and document summaries?
22. What do you need from me to make the relationship work well?

### Agreement, agency and compensation

23. Is your agreement exclusive or nonexclusive, and how long does it last?
24. How can either side terminate it?
25. How are you compensated, and under what scenarios could I owe money directly?
26. What happens if the property is listed by you, your team or another agent at your brokerage?
27. Are there administrative, transaction, marketing or cancellation fees in addition to compensation?
28. Can I review a blank copy of the agreement before deciding?

Do not expect every answer to favor you. A credible agent may explain limits, decline work outside a specialty or recommend another professional. That candor is useful evidence.

## Use this 100-point comparison scorecard

Score each candidate from 1 to 5 in every category. Multiply the score by the listed weight, then divide by 5. Do this immediately after each interview while the differences are fresh.

| Category | Weight | What a high score looks like |
| --- | ---: | --- |
| **Relevant Bend experience** | 20 | Recent work with your side, price range, property type and due-diligence needs. |
| **Property and pricing analysis** | 20 | Uses evidence, explains uncertainty and knows when a specialist or official record is needed. |
| **Process and organization** | 15 | Clear workflow for search or preparation, offers, deadlines, documents, inspections and closing. |
| **Communication and coverage** | 15 | Specific response standards, direct access, reliable backups and a style that fits you. |
| **Agreement and compensation clarity** | 15 | Explains scope, term, exclusivity, termination, fees and possible out-of-pocket scenarios without pressure. |
| **Negotiation judgment** | 10 | Can explain tradeoffs and provide examples without promising an outcome. |
| **Personal fit and candor** | 5 | Listens, challenges assumptions respectfully and is comfortable saying “I need to verify that.” |
| **Total** | **100** | Use the number to expose differences, then apply judgment. |

Do not let a five-point difference create false precision. The scorecard is most useful when it reveals *why* one candidate is stronger. A lower-profile agent with excellent availability and relevant experience may be a better choice than a high-volume name whose work will be delegated.

## Read the Oregon buyer agreement carefully

Oregon's written buyer representation requirements took effect January 1, 2025. For covered residential transactions, a broker or principal broker representing a buyer must enter a written agreement before, or as soon as practicable after, beginning to help identify or purchase property.

The Oregon Real Estate Agency says the agreement must address the following items:

- Whether the relationship is exclusive or nonexclusive.
- The agent's legal obligations, directly or by reference to the Initial Agency Disclosure Pamphlet.
- The licensee's license number.
- Contact information for the supervising principal broker, as applicable.
- Effective and expiration dates; the term, including automatic renewals, cannot exceed 24 months.
- General search criteria, which may include price and location.
- An explanation of compensation.
- Termination rights for the buyer and agent.

### Questions to resolve before signing

Read the actual contract and ask:

1. Does exclusivity cover all residential property, or only a defined area, property type or period?
2. What happens if you already know a seller, builder or off-market opportunity?
3. Does the agreement continue after termination for properties introduced during the term?
4. Can you terminate by written notice, or does the brokerage have to agree?
5. Is there a cancellation fee or reimbursement provision?
6. What compensation is stated, and how is any shortfall handled?
7. Are there separate brokerage, transaction or administrative fees?
8. Does the agreement authorize disclosed limited agency?
9. If a team is involved, which licensed person is responsible for you?
10. What duties are outside the agent's expertise and therefore yours to investigate with other professionals?

You can request time to read a blank agreement before the first private tour. A rushed showing does not require a rushed long-term commitment. If appropriate, ask whether a short-term, property-specific or nonexclusive arrangement is available.

Oregon's Agency also states that a buyer can visit an open house independently without signing a buyer representation agreement merely to enter the open house. If you decide to pursue that property, clarify representation before relying on the listing agent for guidance.

## Understand compensation before you commit

Real estate compensation is negotiable. The important question is not simply, “Does the buyer's agent cost me anything?” The useful question is, “What does my agreement require, what other payment may be available in this transaction and what could I personally owe?”

Ask the agent to show at least three simple examples:

1. The transaction provides enough compensation to cover the amount in your agreement.
2. The available amount is less than the amount in your agreement.
3. No amount is available from another source.

For each example, ask whether you would owe a difference, whether compensation could be negotiated as part of the purchase offer and how that request might affect the offer's economics. Do not rely on a verbal “the seller usually pays” explanation.

Also ask about:

- A flat amount, percentage or other compensation formula.
- Retainers, cancellation charges or transaction fees.
- Whether compensation changes for a new-construction or off-market purchase.
- Referral fees between brokerages.
- Rebates or credits, if offered, and whether lender or escrow approval is required.
- Whether the agreement permits compensation above the stated amount and how any excess is handled.

Compensation and purchase-contract strategy can affect cash needed at closing. Coordinate the answer with your lender and escrow team rather than assuming every credit or concession can be used in the same way. The [Bend closing-cost guide](/buying/closing-costs) explains related budgeting items.

## Know who the agent represents

Representation determines whose interests the agent is serving and what information can be shared.

### Buyer's agent

A buyer's agent represents the buyer under the written agreement and owes the duties described by Oregon law and the Initial Agency Disclosure Pamphlet. That does not turn the agent into an inspector, attorney or title expert; the pamphlet notes limits on investigations outside the agent's expertise.

### Seller's agent

The listing agent represents the seller. A friendly listing agent at an open house can provide access and factual information, but you should not assume that person is advising you as a buyer.

### Disclosed limited agency

Disclosed limited agency can apply when one licensee represents both buyer and seller, when the same real estate business represents both sides, or when a licensee represents competing buyers for the same property. Oregon requires written agreements for the arrangement.

A disclosed limited agent cannot reveal, without written permission, that a seller would accept less, that a buyer would pay more or other protected confidential information. The limitations can extend through the brokerage's supervision structure even when separate agents work with the buyer and seller.

Before consenting, ask:

- Exactly which licensees and principal brokers are limited agents?
- Which individual continues to represent you exclusively, if anyone?
- What pricing, negotiation or confidential advice becomes limited?
- How will the brokerage protect each client's information?
- Can you decline the arrangement and use an agent at another brokerage?

Limited agency is not automatically wrong, but it should be a conscious decision based on the actual structure—not a box you approve without discussion.

## If the agent works on a team

Teams can provide coverage and specialized roles. They can also create confusion if the person who wins your business is not the person doing the day-to-day work.

Oregon added specific real estate team requirements effective in 2026, including a team disclosure before a team member enters a buyer representation or listing agreement. Read it and map the roles.

Ask who will:

- Set strategy and advise on price.
- Attend showings or listing appointments.
- Write and review offers, counteroffers and addenda.
- Track deadlines and coordinate documents.
- Handle inspections, specialists and repair negotiations.
- Communicate during evenings, weekends and vacations.
- Supervise licensed and unlicensed team members.

Ask whether you can contact the lead agent directly when a decision involves material risk. A well-run team should answer these questions easily and put the service model in writing.

## Choosing a Bend listing agent

The best buyer's agent and best listing agent may be different people. Sellers should evaluate the full listing plan, not the highest suggested list price.

### Pricing strategy

Ask for a comparative market analysis and have the agent explain:

- Why each comparable is relevant.
- Adjustments for location, lot, condition, size, views and improvements.
- The difference between list price, pending price and closed price.
- How active competition affects your launch.
- What would trigger a price change and when.
- How the strategy changes if your priority is speed, certainty or maximum exposure.

A high recommended price can be a marketing tactic for winning the listing. The agent should support the number with evidence and explain the risk of accumulating market time.

### Preparation and marketing

Request a written plan covering:

- Repairs, cleaning, staging and what not to spend money on.
- Photography, video, floor plans, aerial media and any limitations.
- Listing copy, property facts and review of data accuracy.
- Launch timing, showing access, open houses and feedback.
- Distribution beyond the local MLS when relevant.
- Marketing for distinctive features without exaggerating or creating fair-housing concerns.
- Who pays for media or staging if the listing is canceled.

Marketing should match the home. More deliverables are not automatically better if they do not help qualified buyers understand the property.

### Offer analysis and transaction management

A listing agent should compare more than price. Ask how the agent presents financing strength, contingencies, appraisal exposure, concessions, closing timeline, possession, buyer-sale dependencies and other risk.

Also ask how inspection negotiations, appraisal issues, title questions and backup offers are handled. The best presentation is a clear net-and-risk comparison, not a stack of offers with a favorite circled.

### Listing agreement

Oregon requires a written listing agreement for a broker or principal broker representing a seller in a covered residential transaction. Review:

- Term and expiration.
- Exclusivity and cancellation.
- Compensation and additional fees.
- Items excluded from or included in the sale.
- Marketing costs and what happens if you terminate.
- Protection periods after expiration.
- Disclosed limited agency authorization.
- Showing, lockbox and offer-presentation procedures.

Interview at least two agents when the property is unusual, the proposed prices differ significantly or the agreement is long and difficult to cancel.

## Match experience to your situation

General transaction volume is less important than relevant experience.

### First-time buyers

Look for patience, education and a repeatable process. The agent should explain documents without making you feel behind, coordinate well with the lender and help you distinguish normal maintenance from material risk. Use the [first-time home buyer guide](/buying/first-time-home-buyers) alongside the agent's advice.

### Relocation buyers

Ask about remote tours, video quality, document sharing, time-zone coverage and local vendor coordination. The agent should help you research objective criteria without choosing a neighborhood on your behalf. If you cannot attend inspections, decide in advance how the agent will participate and what the inspector will document.

### New construction

The builder's sales representative works for the builder. Ask whether your agent has reviewed builder contracts, change-order processes, deposits, completion timelines, orientation inspections and warranty procedures. Confirm when representation must be established; showing up unrepresented at a sales office can limit later options.

### Condos and HOA properties

Look for experience reviewing association documents and coordinating questions about reserves, assessments, insurance, deductibles, restrictions, litigation, maintenance responsibilities and layered associations. An agent should help obtain the documents promptly while making clear when legal, insurance or accounting advice is needed.

### Acreage and homes outside city limits

A Bend mailing address does not guarantee City of Bend services or rules. Ask about experience with wells, septic systems, irrigation, water rights, private roads, access, zoning, wildfire, surveys, outbuildings and county records. A residential city specialist may not be the right fit for a complex rural property.

### Investors and short-term-rental buyers

Do not accept projected revenue without verifying assumptions. You may need land-use, licensing, HOA, tax, financing and management research. Ask the agent to separate known facts from estimates and direct you to the authorities who control the intended use.

### Cash and competitive buyers

Cash does not eliminate due diligence. A strong agent should still explain title, inspection, insurance, appraisal options and the opportunity cost of the offer terms. Speed should come from preparation and organization, not automatic waiver of protections.

## Red flags and weak answers

Be cautious when an agent:

- Pressures you to sign before explaining the agreement.
- Cannot state the term, termination process or compensation in plain English.
- Claims services are “free” without walking through the contract and transaction scenarios.
- Treats years in business or sales volume as the entire value proposition.
- Avoids questions about workload, delegation or backup coverage.
- Promises a price or outcome that cannot be controlled.
- Recommends waiving protections as a default rather than explaining the risk.
- Discourages inspections or specialist follow-up without a property-specific reason.
- Minimizes a concern as “normal for Bend” but offers no evidence or next step.
- Gives legal, structural, environmental, insurance or tax conclusions outside the agent's expertise.
- Uses subjective neighborhood labels instead of objective sources and your criteria.
- Will not provide a blank agreement for review.
- Has inconsistent names, businesses or license details across documents and advertising.
- Wants sensitive documents sent through an unverified channel.
- Speaks poorly of every other professional instead of explaining a constructive process.

Also pay attention to small reliability signals before hiring. Missed calls happen; unexplained patterns matter. If follow-up is disorganized during the sales conversation, service rarely becomes more organized after the contract is signed.

## Make the first working meeting useful

Once you choose an agent, set expectations in writing. A productive first meeting should produce:

- Your goals, timing and major constraints.
- A financing or sale-readiness plan.
- Property or buyer criteria, including acceptable tradeoffs.
- The signed agreement and a plain-language understanding of its terms.
- Communication channels and response expectations.
- Team roles and backup coverage.
- A search or listing-launch plan.
- A list of documents you need to provide.
- A decision framework for offers, inspections and material risk.

For buyers, agree on how quickly listings will be reviewed, who schedules tours and what information the agent will gather before a long drive or remote showing. For sellers, agree on preparation tasks, launch date, showing rules, feedback timing and how offers will be presented.

Revisit expectations if your budget, timeline, target area or property type changes. The agreement and search criteria should match the relationship you are actually using.

## Final decision checklist

Before hiring a Bend real estate agent, confirm all of the following:

- [ ] The Oregon license and registered business are verified.
- [ ] Recent experience matches your transaction and property type.
- [ ] You know who will perform each part of the work.
- [ ] Communication standards and backup coverage are clear.
- [ ] The agent demonstrated an evidence-based pricing or offer process.
- [ ] Bend-specific risks are handled through questions, records and qualified specialists.
- [ ] The representation or listing agreement has been read in full.
- [ ] Term, exclusivity, scope and termination rights are understood.
- [ ] Compensation and every additional fee are understood through real examples.
- [ ] Any team or disclosed limited agency arrangement is understood.
- [ ] The agent welcomes inspections and independent professional advice.
- [ ] You feel comfortable asking a basic question or disagreeing with a recommendation.

The goal is not to find a perfect personality or a person who predicts every market move. It is to hire a licensed professional with relevant experience, a disciplined process, clear incentives and the judgment to help you make better decisions under deadlines.
