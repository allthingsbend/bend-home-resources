## Start here: build an inspection plan, not just an appointment

The biggest mistake is assuming that scheduling a “home inspection” means every important part of the property will be evaluated automatically.

A general inspection is the **first layer**. Depending on the house, your inspection plan may also include a sewer camera, radon test, roof evaluation, structural review, HVAC service inspection, chimney evaluation, septic or well work, moisture investigation or another specialty service.

The right plan depends on the property. A newer townhome on public sewer does not create the same questions as a 1970s house with additions, a steep roof, mature trees and an unfinished crawlspace. A rural Bend-area property with a well, septic system, detached shop and acreage needs a different inspection plan again.

<div class="checklist-box"><strong>Best rule:</strong> if a condition could change whether you buy the house, how much you are willing to pay, or how much cash you need soon after closing, investigate it while your contract still gives you time to act on the answer.</div>

## What an Oregon home inspection actually is

Oregon regulates residential home inspectors through the Construction Contractors Board. An individual who performs a qualifying residential home inspection must be certified, and the inspector must be associated with an appropriately licensed CCB business.

Oregon’s standards are useful because they also make the limitations clear: a home inspection is **visual and not technically exhaustive**. The inspector observes readily visible and accessible components within the scope of the inspection and provides a written report.

A standard inspection can help identify:

- Roof, exterior and drainage conditions visible at the time of inspection.
- Structural components that can be observed without destructive testing.
- Plumbing fixtures, visible supply and drain components and water-heating equipment.
- Electrical service, panels, representative receptacles, fixtures and safety devices within the required scope.
- Heating and central cooling equipment.
- Interiors, windows, doors and built-in kitchen appliances within the inspection scope.
- Insulation, ventilation, attic and crawlspace conditions where accessible.
- Conditions that deserve monitoring, repair, replacement or further evaluation.

### What it is not

Oregon’s standards do **not** require a home inspector to determine everything a buyer may care about. Among other limitations, an inspector is generally not required to determine:

- The remaining life expectancy of every component.
- The exact cause of every defect.
- The repair method or repair cost.
- Code compliance.
- Whether a house is suitable for a specialized use.
- Whether you should or should not buy the property.
- The presence of pests or wood-destroying organisms as part of the basic inspection.
- Underground or concealed conditions.
- Cosmetic items.
- Every detached structure.

That distinction matters. “The inspector did not find it” and “the inspector was required to inspect it” are not the same thing.

Read the inspection agreement before the appointment. Ask what is excluded, which detached structures are included, how the roof will be observed, whether the crawlspace and attic will be entered when accessible, and which add-on services require separate authorization.

## The Bend inspection priority list

Every property is different, but Central Oregon creates a useful set of questions to keep near the top of the list.

### 1. Roofs, flashing and water entry

A roof can look acceptable from the driveway and still have vulnerable details. Ask the inspector to pay particular attention to:

- Roof-covering condition and visible prior repairs.
- Sidewall, headwall, chimney and penetration flashing.
- Pipe boots, skylights and other penetrations.
- Roof-to-wall transitions.
- Gutters and where downspouts discharge.
- Staining, sheathing damage or moisture evidence visible from the attic.
- Snow- and ice-prone areas where roof geometry concentrates runoff.
- Moss, debris or tree contact that may trap moisture or damage materials.

Do not over-focus on a guessed roof age. **Condition, installation details, leak history and remaining serviceability matter more than an unsupported age estimate.**

If the inspector sees active leakage, widespread deterioration, questionable flashing or an inaccessible roof, a specialist evaluation by a licensed roofer may be more valuable than trying to negotiate from a few inspection-report photos. See the [Bend roofer guide](/professionals/roofers) for how to compare that follow-up.

### 2. Drainage, irrigation and moisture

Bend is dry overall, but that does not make water management unimportant. Local homes still experience roof runoff, snowmelt, irrigation leakage, plumbing failures and water concentration against foundations.

Look at the property as a system:

1. Where does roof water land?
2. Where do downspouts discharge?
3. Does hardscape or soil slope toward the structure?
4. Does irrigation spray siding, decks, windows or the foundation area?
5. Are there low areas next to the house?
6. Is there staining, efflorescence, fungal growth, damaged trim or soft wood that suggests recurring moisture?

A moisture meter or thermal camera can help an inspector investigate a suspicious area, but neither is a magic “see through walls” device. If there is credible evidence of concealed damage, the next step may be a moisture specialist, contractor or—in some situations—an invasive inspection authorized by the property owner.

### 3. Crawlspaces and attics

These spaces can tell you more about the house than a freshly painted living room.

In a crawlspace, pay attention to:

- Standing water, damp soil or damaged vapor barrier.
- Plumbing leaks or old repairs.
- Foundation cracking or movement that deserves more analysis.
- Damaged insulation.
- Wood staining, fungal growth or deterioration.
- Improvised structural supports.
- Duct condition where applicable.
- Evidence that animals or pests have entered.

In an attic, look for:

- Roof-deck staining or prior leak evidence.
- Ventilation issues.
- Disconnected bath or dryer exhaust where visible.
- Insulation gaps or unusual modifications.
- Electrical work that appears improvised.
- Framing changes around remodels, chimneys, skylights or mechanical equipment.

If the inspector cannot enter a crawlspace or attic because of access, stored belongings, unsafe conditions, snow, insulation or other restrictions, treat that as **remaining uncertainty**, not as a clean bill of health.

### 4. Siding, trim, decks and exposed wood

Central Oregon sun, snow, irrigation and seasonal temperature changes are hard on exposed materials.

Inspect:

- Siding clearance from soil, roofs and hardscape.
- Damaged or missing flashing at wall transitions.
- Soft or deteriorated trim near windows, doors and roof intersections.
- Deck ledger connections and visible framing.
- Guardrails and stairs.
- Posts and framing near grade.
- Paint or coating failure that exposes wood.
- Areas where a roof drains directly onto a deck or wall.

A small patch of soft trim may be simple. Repeated deterioration in the same location can be evidence of a water-management problem that needs to be solved before cosmetic repair.

### 5. Heating, cooling and freeze protection

Do not settle for “the furnace turned on.” Ask for the equipment type, approximate age when it can be supported, visible condition and what was actually operated.

For Bend homes, useful questions include:

- Is the primary heating system operating normally under the conditions of the inspection?
- Is there a heat pump, furnace, boiler, mini-split or combination system?
- Are there rooms with noticeably different heating or cooling?
- Are exhaust and combustion components visibly appropriate?
- Is service history available?
- Are exterior hose bibs, exposed piping or irrigation components vulnerable to freezing?
- If the property is vacant or winterized, what could not be tested?

A home inspector is not a substitute for an HVAC technician when the system is malfunctioning, unusually old, making abnormal noise or showing signs of unsafe operation.

### 6. Electrical systems and remodel history

Older homes may have decades of additions and repairs layered on top of the original system.

Look beyond whether the lights turn on. Important questions include:

- Service size and panel condition.
- Evidence of overheating or improper modifications.
- Grounding and bonding observations within the inspection scope.
- GFCI/AFCI protection where evaluated under Oregon standards.
- Representative receptacle and fixture operation.
- Visible older wiring or mixed wiring systems.
- Subpanels serving additions, garages, shops or hot tubs.
- Electrical work associated with remodeled kitchens, bathrooms or additions.

If the report identifies a material panel, wiring or grounding concern, get an electrician’s evaluation rather than relying on a generic repair allowance.

### 7. Plumbing, water heaters and hidden leak clues

A visual plumbing inspection cannot see every pipe in a wall or underground. It can still identify patterns that deserve follow-up.

Pay attention to:

- Water pressure or flow concerns observed during normal fixture operation.
- Active leaks and corrosion.
- Drain performance at multiple fixtures.
- Water-heater age, condition, venting and safety components that are within scope.
- Plumbing materials that may influence future maintenance.
- Evidence of prior supply-line or drain repairs.
- Staining beneath bathrooms, kitchens, laundry areas and mechanical rooms.
- Exterior hose bibs and shutoffs.

Ask where the home’s main water shutoff is and whether it was accessible. Knowing that location on day one of ownership can prevent a small leak from becoming a large one.

### 8. Retaining walls, slopes and site conditions

Many Bend properties are not flat. Hillside lots, terraced landscaping and retaining walls can add both value and complexity.

A general inspector may note visible movement, leaning, cracking or drainage concerns, but a home inspection is not an engineering analysis. A structural or geotechnical professional may be appropriate when a retaining wall is large, materially displaced, supporting a structure or driveway, or associated with slope movement.

Also note where runoff from uphill property travels during snowmelt or storms. A dry August inspection cannot show every wet-season condition.

### 9. Wildfire-related vulnerabilities

A home inspection is not a complete wildfire-risk assessment and does not tell you whether a property will be insurable. Still, the inspection is a useful time to note visible vulnerabilities that may become ownership projects:

- Vegetation or combustible material directly against structures.
- Debris accumulation on roofs or in gutters.
- Wood fencing or decking attached to the house.
- Vulnerable vents or openings.
- Dead trees or limbs near structures.
- Outbuildings and stored combustible material near the home.

For newer work, also verify applicable permit records rather than assuming visible construction meets current wildfire-hardening requirements.

## Which add-on inspections are worth considering?

Do not buy every add-on automatically. Match the service to the property and the question you need answered.

| Service | What it can help answer | When it deserves serious consideration |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Sewer scope** | What is happening inside the underground sewer lateral? | Public-sewer homes where the lateral condition is unknown, especially with mature trees, older piping, slow drains or prior sewer work. |
| **Radon test** | What is the radon level in this specific home during the test period? | Oregon Health Authority recommends testing during the buying inspection process; area risk maps cannot determine an individual home’s level. |
| **Roof evaluation** | Is a suspected roof defect repairable, and what is the likely scope? | Active leaks, widespread wear, questionable flashing, inaccessible roofing or conflicting opinions. |
| **Structural engineer** | Is observed movement structurally significant and what further work is appropriate? | Significant foundation movement, altered framing, large retaining-wall issues, unusual deflection or major cracking. |
| **Mold/moisture investigation** | Is there an active moisture source, and is sampling or invasive investigation justified? | Recurring leaks, visible growth, musty odor, elevated moisture or concealed-damage concerns. |
| **HVAC specialist** | What is the actual mechanical condition beyond basic operation? | Malfunction, abnormal noise, combustion concern, very old equipment or major replacement risk. |
| **Chimney/fireplace specialist** | Are flue and fireplace components safe and serviceable beyond a basic visual review? | Frequent wood-burning use, older masonry, visible cracking, prior chimney work or unknown service history. |
| **Septic inspection** | What is the condition and operation of the onsite wastewater system? | Any property served by septic. Confirm the appropriate inspection scope with Deschutes County requirements and a qualified provider. |
| **Well / water testing** | Is the private water system operating properly and what does the water test show? | Any property served by a private well or shared water system. |
| **Pest / WDO inspection** | Is there evidence of wood-destroying organisms or related damage? | Visible insect activity, damaged wood, damp crawlspaces, lender requirements or property-specific concern. |
| **Lead / asbestos testing** | Are specific suspect materials present? | Older homes where planned renovation or suspicious materials make the answer decision-relevant. Testing is separate from a general visual inspection. |

A specialist should answer a **specific question**. “Further evaluation recommended” is not the finish line; ask what needs to be evaluated, by whom and why.

## Sewer scopes: one of the easiest blind spots to understand

A standard home inspection does not inspect an underground sewer lateral. That makes the sewer line a simple example of why add-on services exist.

A sewer camera inspection may identify conditions such as:

- Root intrusion.
- Cracks or deterioration.
- Separated or offset joints.
- Standing water or a significant belly.
- Obstructions.
- Evidence of prior repairs.
- A line that cannot be fully scoped, which is itself useful information.

This matters in Bend because the City’s current design standards state that sewer services are owned by the property owner they serve and that the property owner is responsible for maintenance of the sewer service from the main into the property.

That does **not** mean every Bend buyer needs a sewer scope. It means the line can create a meaningful homeowner expense, while remaining invisible during the normal inspection.

Questions to ask the sewer-scope provider:

1. Where did the camera enter the line?
2. How far did it travel?
3. Did it reach the city main, HOA connection or septic tank as applicable?
4. What pipe material was visible?
5. Were roots, offsets, cracks, standing water or obstructions observed?
6. Can I receive the video, not just a one-line summary?
7. If there is a problem, what specialist should evaluate the repair options?

If the scope shows a material defect, get the repair approach and price from the appropriate licensed contractor before assuming a worst-case number.

## Radon: test the house, not the ZIP code

The Oregon Health Authority recommends that buyers test a home for radon during the inspection process. Radon testing is not required simply because a home is being sold, but a buyer can request testing.

OHA’s Oregon radon data classifies Bend ZIP codes differently—some low, some moderate—and explicitly warns that maps are **not a substitute for testing an individual home**. Results can vary significantly between nearby houses.

That makes the decision straightforward: if you want to know the radon level in the house you are buying, test that house using an appropriate method.

OHA says radon is measured in picocuries per liter and references the EPA action level of **4.0 pCi/L**. Follow the testing protocol carefully, especially during a real-estate transaction where closed-house conditions and device placement matter.

## Check the permit history while you are inspecting the physical house

An inspection tells you what is physically visible. Permit research helps answer a different question: **what work does the jurisdiction have on record?**

For property inside Bend city limits, the City says its Permit Lookup Tool can be used to explore historical permits dating back to **1993** for a tax lot. For earlier records, the City directs users to Permit Center staff.

That makes permit research especially useful when you see:

- Additions or converted garages.
- Finished basements or attic spaces.
- New decks or major structural work.
- Electrical panels or extensive rewiring.
- Added bathrooms or kitchens.
- HVAC changes.
- Roof work.
- ADUs.
- Large retaining walls.
- Recent remodels that do not match the apparent age of the house.

For property outside Bend’s city jurisdiction, use the applicable Deschutes County and Oregon ePermitting resources instead of assuming City of Bend records are complete.

### What if you cannot find a permit?

A missing record is a **question**, not automatic proof that work was illegal or unsafe. Records can be incomplete, older work may predate online systems, the address may have changed, or the work may not have required the permit you expected.

Ask:

1. What work appears to have been performed?
2. When was it done?
3. Was a permit required at that time?
4. Can the seller provide invoices, plans, final inspection records or contractor information?
5. Does the jurisdiction have an older or differently indexed record?
6. Does the physical work raise a safety or structural concern regardless of paperwork?

The permit file and the inspection report should inform each other; neither replaces the other.

## How much does a Bend home inspection cost?

There is no official Bend “average,” and a cheap inspection can become expensive if the scope is too narrow for the property.

Published pricing from Bend/Central Oregon providers checked in August 2026 illustrates the range:

These are **examples, not a market quote or endorsement**. Pricing changes, and another inspector may structure fees differently.

Expect the total to vary based on:

- Square footage.
- Age and complexity.
- Detached garages, shops, ADUs or other structures.
- Crawlspace and roof access.
- Travel distance.
- Sewer scope.
- Radon testing.
- Well or septic services.
- Other specialized testing.

If you are comparing two inspectors, ask both for the **total price for the same property and same requested services**. A $75 difference in the base inspection is not meaningful if one quote includes a detached garage, crawlspace entry and sewer scope while the other does not.

## What to do before inspection day

A little preparation makes the appointment far more useful.

### Send the inspector what you already know

Share material information from the listing and [seller disclosures](/buying/seller-disclosures), especially:

- Known leaks or water damage.
- Roof repairs or replacement claims.
- Additions or remodels.
- Foundation or drainage work.
- Sewer or septic history.
- Insurance-related repairs.
- Recent electrical, plumbing or HVAC work.
- Areas you noticed during showings.

Do not worry about “biasing” the inspector by telling them where you saw a ceiling stain. The goal is to investigate the house, not conduct a blind test.

### Confirm access

Ask your agent or seller-side contact to make sure the inspector can reach:

- Electrical panels.
- Attic hatches.
- Crawlspace entries.
- Water heater and HVAC equipment.
- Under-sink plumbing.
- Garages and included outbuildings.
- The sewer cleanout if a scope is scheduled.

A room packed floor-to-ceiling with belongings or a blocked crawlspace entry can leave an important part of the property uninspected.

### Bring your own short list

You do not need a 100-item DIY inspection sheet. Write down the property-specific questions that matter most to you:

- “What caused the stain in the primary bedroom ceiling?”
- “Can you tell whether this deck ledger is visible and properly connected?”
- “The seller disclosed a plumbing leak in 2023—do you see remaining moisture damage?”
- “This addition does not appear in the listing square footage. Is there anything visible that concerns you?”
- “The roof is listed as 18 years old. What is its actual observed condition?”

Use the [Bend home inspection checklist](/tools/home-inspection-checklist) as an organizer, not a substitute for the professional inspection.

## Should you attend the inspection?

If you can, yes.

You usually do not need to follow the inspector from room to room for the entire appointment. In fact, giving them space to work can be more productive. But being present for the final walkthrough or summary lets you see the most important issues in context.

Ask these questions before you leave:

1. **What are the three to five findings you would prioritize if you were buying this house?**
2. **What could you not inspect or access?**
3. **Which findings are safety issues?**
4. **Which findings may involve active water or ongoing damage?**
5. **Which items need specialist evaluation before my contingency ends?**
6. **Which items are ordinary maintenance rather than transaction-level problems?**
7. **Is there anything in the report that will sound scarier in writing than it looked in person?**

The last question is underrated. Inspection reports are intentionally thorough and can make a serviceable house sound catastrophic when every minor deficiency is read with equal weight.

## How to read a 60-page inspection report without panicking

Do not start by counting defects. A house with 72 comments is not necessarily worse than a house with 38 comments; inspectors document differently.

Sort findings into four buckets.

### Bucket 1: safety or active damage

Examples can include:

- Active water intrusion.
- Electrical hazards.
- Unsafe combustion or venting concerns.
- Significant structural movement.
- Conditions that create immediate fall or life-safety risk.

These deserve prompt attention and often specialist follow-up.

### Bucket 2: potentially expensive unknowns

These are often the most important transaction items because the inspection cannot answer the final question yet.

Examples:

- “Roof appears near end of service life; roofer evaluation recommended.”
- “Foundation movement observed; engineer recommended.”
- “Moisture detected behind shower wall; source not determined.”
- “Sewer camera could not pass obstruction.”
- “Heating system did not operate properly.”

The goal is to convert an unknown into better information before deciding how to negotiate.

### Bucket 3: deferred maintenance

These items matter, but many are normal ownership projects:

- Aging caulk.
- Minor siding maintenance.
- Deck refinishing.
- Gutter cleaning or extensions.
- Small plumbing drips.
- Weatherstripping.
- Routine HVAC service.

Add them to a first-year maintenance budget rather than treating every one as a reason to renegotiate the deal.

### Bucket 4: cosmetic or preference items

Paint color, worn finishes, dated fixtures and other visible cosmetic conditions are usually things you already saw when making the offer. Keep them separate from newly discovered property defects.

<div class="checklist-box"><strong>Think in dollars and uncertainty, not defect count.</strong> One unresolved foundation question can matter more than 30 loose handles, cracked switch plates and missing door stops.</div>

## When the inspector recommends a specialist

“Recommend further evaluation” is frustrating when you are on a deadline, but it often means the inspector is staying within the appropriate professional scope.

Use this process:

1. **Clarify the concern.** Ask what the inspector observed and what question remains unanswered.
2. **Choose the right specialist.** Roofer for a roof system, electrician for material electrical concerns, engineer for structural questions, and so on.
3. **Give the specialist the report photos and exact location.** Do not make them rediscover the issue from scratch.
4. **Ask for a written conclusion.** Is repair needed? How urgent is it? What is the reasonable repair scope?
5. **Get a price when price affects the deal.** An inspection recommendation alone is not a repair estimate.
6. **Keep the timeline visible.** Coordinate with your agent so you understand your contractual deadlines.

If the specialist says “replace everything” and the economics are significant, a second opinion can be worthwhile—particularly when the person diagnosing the issue is also selling the replacement.

## How to negotiate inspection findings without turning the report into a wish list

Your contract and agent should guide the actual negotiation strategy. From a property-analysis standpoint, it helps to focus on material information that became clearer during due diligence.

A practical priority order is:

1. **Health and safety issues.**
2. **Active leaks or conditions causing continuing damage.**
3. **Structural issues.**
4. **Failed or near-failed major systems.**
5. **Expensive concealed or underground problems confirmed by specialists.**
6. **Major work that appears materially different from what was represented.**
7. **Significant deferred maintenance you did not reasonably know about when offering.**
8. **Small maintenance and cosmetic items.**

Possible outcomes may include seller repair, a credit where permitted, a price adjustment, additional investigation, accepting the condition, or—in some transactions—deciding not to proceed. The best option depends on the contract, financing, seller, property and your own tolerance for post-closing projects.

### Repair request or credit?

A seller repair can make sense when the work is straightforward and must be completed before closing. A credit can give the buyer more control over contractor selection and final scope, but lender and closing rules may limit how credits can be structured or used.

Whichever path you take, avoid vague language such as “repair roof.” A useful repair agreement identifies the condition, required scope or qualified trade, documentation and completion standard.

## Red flag, yellow flag or normal house problem?

Not every expensive item is a reason to walk away, and not every cheap item is harmless.

| Type | How to think about it |
| --- | --- |
| **Red flag** | The issue materially affects safety, structural integrity, habitability or the economics of the purchase, and the uncertainty cannot be reduced enough within your timeline. |
| **Yellow flag** | The issue may be significant, but a specialist can define the scope and cost well enough for you to make a rational decision. |
| **Normal ownership item** | The condition is visible, manageable and reasonably budgetable as maintenance or an improvement. |

For example, an older roof is not automatically a red flag if it is currently serviceable and you have budgeted for eventual replacement. A small recurring leak hidden behind siding can be more concerning if nobody can determine the extent of concealed damage.

## Special situations that deserve a different inspection plan

### New construction

New does not mean defect-free. Consider a professional inspection before closing and, where appropriate, an additional inspection before the builder-warranty period ends. Verify that required jurisdiction inspections and final approvals are complete; a private inspector and a municipal code inspection serve different purposes.

### Condos and townhomes

Clarify what belongs to the unit versus the association. The inspector may evaluate accessible components of your unit while roofs, exterior walls, common plumbing, decks, crawlspaces or other systems may be maintained by the HOA.

Review the governing documents, reserve information, recent meeting minutes and known building projects alongside the unit inspection. A pristine interior does not tell you the financial condition of the shared exterior systems.

### Rural or edge-of-Bend property

Expand the plan when the property includes:

- Private well or shared water.
- Septic system.
- Propane tank.
- Long private driveway.
- Large detached shop or barn.
- Irrigation rights or equipment.
- Private road or bridge.
- Acreage, slopes or drainage features.

Confirm which items your general inspector will cover and which require separate providers.

### Older homes

An older house is not automatically worse than a newer one, but expect more layers of change. Pay attention to:

- Electrical updates.
- Plumbing replacements.
- Foundation modifications.
- Additions and enclosed porches.
- Older windows and insulation.
- Chimneys and fireplaces.
- Sewer materials.
- Lead- or asbestos-containing materials where testing is decision-relevant.
- Permit history for major renovations.

The best older-home inspection is not one that declares everything “old.” It distinguishes durable original systems, normal aging, questionable alterations and actual failure.

## The final step: turn the report into an ownership plan

After the transaction decision is made, keep the inspection report. It can become your first maintenance roadmap.

Create three lists:

### Before move-in

Safety items, active leaks, security issues, major cleaning, utility setup and anything easier to complete in an empty house.

### First year

Deferred maintenance, drainage improvements, service appointments, minor exterior repairs and projects that prevent larger problems.

### Monitor

Aging but functional systems, small cracks or movement your specialist says to watch, older appliances and future capital expenses.

Then use the [final walkthrough guide](/buying/final-walkthrough) to confirm that the home’s condition has not materially changed before closing and that any agreed repairs are documented appropriately.

## A strong inspection outcome is not “nothing wrong”

Every house has defects and maintenance needs. The best outcome is that you finish due diligence with a much smaller list of **unknowns**.

You should be able to answer:

- What are the largest likely expenses in the next few years?
- Is there active damage happening now?
- Which systems are old but functional versus actually failing?
- What could not be inspected?
- Which specialist recommendations were resolved?
- Are there material permit or remodel questions still open?
- Is the sewer, septic or well situation understood?
- Do the repair needs still fit your budget and tolerance for projects?

If you can answer those questions, the inspection has done its job—even if the report is long.
